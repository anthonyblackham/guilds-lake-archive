## 1. The promoter

<p class="when">1857-1904</p>

::: figure ch1-portrait.jpg small
**Figure {ch1-portrait}.** "Hon. Lafe Pence", from the Sigma Chi fraternity's quarterly; the "Hon." dates it to his
years in Congress or soon after. The Sigma Chi Quarterly, vol. 12, p. 123.
[Catalogue entry](cat:sigma-chi-quarterly-v12-pence-portrait)
:::

Lafayette Pence (Figure {ch1-portrait}) was born in Columbus, Indiana, in December 1857. He went west as a lawyer, settled in Denver in 1885,
and moved quickly through Colorado politics: the state House of Representatives, then two years as prosecutor for
Arapahoe County. In 1892 he rode the Populist wave into Congress, and he sat for one term, from March 1893 to March
1895, before losing his seat. After that came a string of ventures: railroads in New York, a law practice in
Washington, and hydraulic mining at Breckenridge, Colorado, the same method, giants washing down gravel banks, that he
would bring to Portland (Biographical Directory of the U.S. Congress).

By 1904 he was a promoter at large in the Northwest. In February he was among the incorporators of a canal company at
Ontario, in eastern Oregon
([Oregonian, 7 Feb 1904](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1904-02-07-p7-pence-ontario-canal)); by summer, "of Colorado", he
was offering Lewiston, Idaho, a gravity water system from the Sweetwater country
([Journal, 1 Aug 1904](cat:news-oregon-journal-1904-08-01-p6-pence-lewiston)). He left a trail of small debts behind
him: a hotel in Salt Lake City cashed his $90 draft on Lewiston, and it came back marked "no funds"
([Journal, 27 Aug 1904](cat:news-oregon-journal-1904-08-27-p1-pence-warrant-utah-angry)).

Portland met him in August 1904, at the American Mining Congress. Speaking for Denver against Salt Lake City as the
congress's permanent home, he told the delegates it should be "a mining bureau and not a matrimonial bureau", a jibe at
the Mormons that set off an uproar
([Oregonian, 26 Aug 1904](cat:news-oregonian-1904-08-26-p1-pence-mining-congress-stir)). The Utah delegates demanded an
apology, Idaho's leaders, W. E. Borah among them, wired that he did not speak for Idaho
([Journal, 26 Aug 1904](cat:news-oregon-journal-1904-08-26-p2-pence-mining-congress-uproar)), and Denver won the vote
anyway ([Journal, 26 Aug 1904](cat:news-oregon-journal-1904-08-26-p1-denver-wins-mining-congress)). The next day Salt
Lake issued a warrant against him over the $90 draft, and a Utah judge called him "a soldier of fortune"
([Journal, 27 Aug 1904](cat:news-oregon-journal-1904-08-27-p1-pence-warrant-utah-angry)). Pence said he had drawn the
draft on himself, expecting money from his Lewiston deals, and would pay
([Journal, 28 Aug 1904](cat:news-oregon-journal-1904-08-28-p3-pence-explains-salt-lake-debt)); he went to Salt Lake to
face what he called "the rawest sort of spitework"
([Oregonian, 30 Aug 1904](cat:news-oregonian-1904-08-30-p7-pence-goes-to-salt-lake)).

Within weeks he was back at the Portland hotel, saying he had given up politics for business and urging the city to
court the Lewiston country ([Oregonian, 15 Sep 1904](cat:news-oregonian-1904-09-15-p5-pence-idaho-tributary)). The
Lewis and Clark Exposition was going up on the shore of Guild's Lake, and the hills behind it were full of water and
gravel.

<div class="keynum"><span>One term</span> in Congress, 1893-95; then ten years of railroads, mining and water schemes
before Portland.</div>

## 2. The big idea

<p class="when">1905</p>

Pence's scheme began with water. In February 1905 he filed on Bull Run, Portland's own water source, for a suburban
water company; the legislature promptly passed a bill that no one but the city could claim Bull Run water, and he
withdrew ([Journal, 14 Feb 1905](cat:news-oregon-journal-1905-02-14-p8-pence-bull-run-filing)). In March he and two
partners incorporated the West Portland Canal & Pipe Line Co.
([Journal, 9 Mar 1905](cat:news-oregon-journal-1905-03-09-p16-west-portland-canal-pipe-line-inc)), and over the
spring he recorded claims on creek after creek round the city: Marquam, Tanner, Balch and Johnson creeks, and in
Washington County 125 cubic feet a second of East Dairy Creek and 75 of McKay Creek, to be brought 20 miles by canal,
with six reservoirs, at a cost put at a million dollars. The purposes named included hydraulic mining, sluicing and
filling ([Oregonian, 13 Apr 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-04-13-p8-pence-water-filings-hawkins)).

The idea was to wash the West Hills into Guild's Lake. Water from the far side of the hills would come round by canal
and tunnel to the crest above Willamette Heights, drop to hydraulic giants, and sluice the hillsides down into the low
ground, making building terraces on the hills and new land in the lake (Figure {ch2-ditch-1905})
([Oregonian, 21 May 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-05-21-p31)). By April his men had built nearly four miles of benches
behind the Heights and laid 2,800 feet of flume lumber, with 42 men in camp
([Hillsboro Independent, 21 Apr 1905](cat:news-hillsboro-independent-1905-04-21-p4-will-tear-hills-down)). The
Scottish-American Investment Co., which owned much of the hill land, backed him
([Oregonian, 16 Apr 1905](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1905-04-16-p10-blyth-ultimatum-park-board)).

::: figure ch2-ditch-1905.jpg
**Figure {ch2-ditch-1905}.** "Lafe Pence's Ditch and What He Purposes to Accomplish." The diagram looks west from
over Willamette Heights: the headgate on Balch Creek, the canal and flume under construction along the canyon, the
proposed tunnels, and Sylvan, 775 feet above the lake, at the far end; the photographs show the headgate, the canal
being dug on the crest above Macleay Park, the lower flume, the upper flume, and the hill to be cut down. Sunday
Oregonian, 21 May 1905, p. 31. [Catalogue entry](cat:news-oregonian-1905-05-21-p31)
:::

The trouble was the park. The surest way down to the hill lay through Macleay Park, and its commissioner, Colonel
L. L. Hawkins, fought him from the start. In April the city attorney warned that anyone carrying Balch Creek water over
city land would be arrested for trespass
([Journal, 15 Apr 1905](cat:news-oregon-journal-1905-04-15-p8-pence-warned-balch-gulch)), and Hawkins, finding that
Pence's surveyed ditch already cut across a corner of the park, posted a man to arrest him at the first shovelful
([Oregonian, 15 Apr 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-04-15-p10-trespass-stops-pence)). The county judge called the scheme
"the airiest I have ever seen"
([Oregonian, 28 Apr 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-04-28-p11-webster-no-water-rights)). In May Pence took the Park
Board, the mayor and the city engineer along his ditch, 600 feet above the city, and explained how the water would come
from Dairy Creek by tunnels under Sylvan and the Barnes road (Figure {ch2-works-map})
([Oregonian, 9 May 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-05-09-p16)); the board split two to two
([Oregonian, 10 May 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-05-10-p11-park-board-postpones-pence)). The next day one of his
teamsters, James Dixson, was killed when blasting on the ditch stampeded his team
([Journal, 10 May 1905](cat:news-oregon-journal-1905-05-10-p3-pence-teamster-injured-blast)).

::: figure ch2-works-map.jpg
**Figure {ch2-works-map}.** How the works were laid out by 1906. Top: the Pence Company's own "Map showing the general
course of the Pence Company High Line Flume and the Pence Company Canal", drawn on the township and section grid.
Bottom: the same ground today, in the same frame, with the two routes traced from it: the canal from its intakes on the
Washington County creeks, the High Line Flume (dashed), the crossing at Cornelius Pass and the long run south along the

Macleay Park. Base map © OpenStreetMap contributors. [Catalogue entry](cat:pdx-archives-a2009-009-3473)
:::

On 1 June the Lewis and Clark Exposition opened on the lake shore below. Pence, who had borrowed $500 that morning to
pay his men and give each a five-dollar gold piece to spend at the fair, watched the opening from the hill with his son
([Journal, 8 Mar 1907](cat:news-oregon-journal-1907-03-08-p5-pence-filling-terminals-35-miles-flumes)). The Board of
Trade endorsed his plan for terracing Willamette Heights
([Oregonian, 3 Jun 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-06-03-p14-park-board-hears-pence-board-of-trade)), but on 15 June the
Park Board refused him, unanimously, any flume or pipe through Macleay Park and any use of Balch Creek
([Journal, 15 Jun 1905](cat:news-oregon-journal-1905-06-15-p8-park-board-refuses-pence-oregon-buildings)). The
Oregonian said the refusal was no judgment on his scheme, only respect for Donald Macleay's wish that the park be kept
wild ([Oregonian, 17 Jun 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-06-17-p8-editorial-macleay-wish-respected)). Within days his
backing fell through; he laid off his unpaid men and contracted out the finishing of the upper ditch
([Oregonian, 25 Jun 1905](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1905-06-25-p24-touch-of-high-finance)). He would come back to the
park that winter, without asking.

<div class="keynum"><span>20 miles</span> of canal planned, from Dairy Creek in Washington County to the hills behind
Willamette Heights; four miles of benches built by April 1905.</div>

## 3. The flume war

<p class="when">December 1905 - March 1906</p>

Pence needed a way through Macleay Park. Balch Creek ran down its canyon, and the hill he meant to wash away stood just
beyond it, behind Willamette Heights. In December 1905 he said he would ask the Mayor and the Park Board for leave to run
a flume through the park for three years, at a rental
([Oregonian, 18 Dec 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-12-18-p8-lafe-pence-buys-buildings)). The Oregonian warned that if
he laid violent hands on the park he would have "the fight of his life"
([Oregonian, 19 Dec 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-12-19-p8-editorial-fight-of-his-life)). The new mayor, Harry Lane,
walked the ground with him and told him to get the board's consent and give bond. Pence never applied
([Oregonian, 1 Mar 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-03-01-p16-mayor-lane-explains-flume)).

He built it anyway. Over the winter his crews put up more than 6,000 feet of plank box flume on trestles along the north
wall of the canyon (Figure {ch3-park-flume-map}), known, he said later, only to "a trusted few". His reasoning was that a working flume would make a
better argument than a request, which would only be turned down
([Oregonian, 27 Feb 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-02-27-p10-truce-may-end-fight-of-flume)). On Saturday 24 February
Lane found it carrying water across city property. The next afternoon he came back with Captain Bruin and a squad of
police carrying sledgehammers, smashed a stretch of it at the upper end of the park, and left officers to guard
the wreck overnight (Figure {ch3-broken-flume})
([Oregonian, 26 Feb 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-02-26-p1-smashes-flume-of-lafe-pence)). The upper end was where
the flume left its intake on Balch Creek and entered the park, and later accounts say a survey found the smashed
stretch lay just outside the park line; no report of that survey from 1906 has turned up. Pence answered that his
company could condemn what it needed, and that four giants were at work on Balch Creek water
([Oregonian, 26 Feb 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-02-26-p2-smashes-pence-flume-continued-pence-story)).

Then it turned into a truce. On Monday Lane, the city attorney, the city engineer and a park commissioner waded the full
length of the flume with Pence in pelting rain, Pence in rubber boots and a cowboy hat, and came out to see "the big
hydraulic giant at work on the hillside back of Willamette Heights" (Figure {ch3-giants})
([Oregonian, 27 Feb 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-02-27-p10-truce-may-end-fight-of-flume)). On Tuesday the mayor told
the Park Board that Portland always drove its promoters off to Seattle, and the board voted two to one to let the flume
cross the park for a year from 15 May: water only in the rainy months, a bond against damage, removal when asked, and
no claim to the creek
([Journal, 27 Feb 1906](cat:news-oregon-journal-1906-02-27-p1-park-board-lets-pence-build-flume)). Within 35 minutes
water was running through the mended flume again, and the giants worked day and night
([Oregonian, 1 Mar 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-03-01-p10-eating-away-the-hill-birdseye)).

::: figure ch3-giants.jpg
**Figure {ch3-giants}.** "Hydraulic giants attacking a hill on Portland Heights, near Macleay Park": the raw cut face
at left, the sluiced floor, and two giants on their mounts (marked X). This is the Scotch Nubbin's back face, a year
before the season described in chapter 5. Morning Oregonian, 27 February 1906, p. 10.
[Catalogue entry](cat:news-oregonian-1906-02-27-p10-truce-may-end-fight-of-flume)
:::

The paper now explained the works with a bird's-eye drawing (Figure {ch3-birdseye}). An upper flume came round
the head of the basin through a tunnel and dropped its water 324 feet in a pipe to one high-pressure giant, which did
the cutting; the park flume fed two more that swept the spoil down Balch Gulch toward the lake. About 60 feet had been
cut into the hill so far, and the plan was to turn it into three terraces for houses
([Oregonian, 1 Mar 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-03-01-p10-eating-away-the-hill-birdseye)). The giants stood in the
small valley behind the knoll and worked at its back face, where the park flume ended at a headgate and the upper flume's pipe came
down to them (Figure {ch3-park-flume-map}). Pence invited the public
out to watch: take the W car across the Thurman Street bridge to Rugby Street and walk up the brush road
([Journal, 1 Mar 1906](cat:news-oregon-journal-1906-03-01-p14-pence-invites-public-hydraulic-work)). On 2 March the
board approved the written contract, a cash bond and a railed footwalk along the top of the flume included
([Journal, 2 Mar 1906](cat:news-oregon-journal-1906-03-02-p9-pence-at-peace-with-city-contract)). "When it comes to
handling Park Boards," the Oregonian remarked, "Lafe Pence is right there"
([Oregonian, 1 Mar 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-03-01-p8-quip-pence-handling-park-boards)).

::: figure ch3-birdseye.jpg

<div class="keynum"><span>6,000 feet</span> of flume built through a city park without permission, smashed by the
mayor, and licensed by the Park Board two days later.</div>

## 4. The fair, bought for scrap

<p class="when">December 1905 - 1906</p>

When the exposition closed, its state buildings were sold off for whatever they would bring, and Pence bought most of
them. In mid-December he moved his company's offices into the fair's Emergency Hospital and announced that, for Eastern
capitalists, he was buying the State Commission's buildings to wreck them for flume lumber
([Oregonian, 18 Dec 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-12-18-p8-lafe-pence-buys-buildings)). On 28 December he paid the
last instalment: $2,725 for the Agricultural, Oriental, Oregon State and Auditorium buildings, the fire station, the
police headquarters and the Colonnade
([Oregonian, 29 Dec 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-12-29-p11-pence-pays-final-installment)). The California and
Washington buildings followed at a few hundred dollars each
([Oregonian, 30 Dec 1905](cat:news-oregonian-1905-12-30-p10-lafe-pence-buyer)). The Journal worked out that the
Commission's buildings had cost $290,000 to put up and had been sold for $5,525
([Journal, 31 Dec 1905](cat:news-oregon-journal-1905-12-31-p12-buildings-cost-290000-sold-5525)) (Figure
{ch4-buildings-map}).

::: figure ch4-buildings-map.jpg
**Figure {ch4-buildings-map}.** The fair, bought for scrap. Coloured: the buildings Pence bought in December 1905 from
the State Commission and others, and the Government Building and its annexes on the peninsula, bought in May 1906; the
Emergency Hospital, his office, is outlined. Grey: the rest of the fair. The labels for the Cottel tract and the fill
behind the Government Building show roughly where the papers put them; neither is mapped from a survey. Made for this
story from the 1905 Sanborn map's building footprints and the 1902-03 surveys of the grounds.
:::

He settled in as lord of the empty fair. In January he and Mrs. Pence moved into the rooms Governor Pardee had used in
the California building, with his flume crew lodged in the hospital, all on behalf of a new Lewis and Clark Wrecking
Company ([Oregonian, 22 Jan 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-01-22-p13-pences-dwell-in-california-building)). He fenced
the grounds against fire, then let Sunday visitors back in under police guard
([Oregonian, 5 Feb 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-02-05-p8-wreck-of-the-fair-sunday-visitors)). Wrecking began in
earnest in March, and the big timbers were to go into his flumes
([Oregonian, 15 Mar 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-03-15-p12-wrecking-work-is-begun)).

The land was the real prize. In January he was negotiating for about 80 acres of the Cottle estate, part of it under
Guild's Lake, to fill with the spoil of the hills he had contracts to level
([Oregonian, 16 Jan 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-01-16-p11-pence-would-fill-part-of-lake-cottle)). In February,
through the Portland Trust Co., the Fleischner, Hirsch, Goldsmith and Mayer tracts of the fairground went for some
$390,000, reportedly for a railroad terminal
([Journal, 10 Feb 1906](cat:news-oregon-journal-1906-02-10-p6-pence-buys-fairground-tracts)), and in March it came out
that John S. McMillin of Seattle, through his Portland Development Co., had bought about 70 acres and twelve fair
buildings to turn into factories: a project, the Oregonian said, "engineered by Mr. Lafe Pence"
([Oregonian, 25 Mar 1906](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1906-03-25-p1-fair-site-sold-for-factories-mcmillin);
[Oregonian, 25 Mar 1906](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1906-03-25-p6-editorial-white-citys-future)). Pence said McMillin was
an old college friend from Indiana.

::: figure ch4-perspective-1906.jpg
**Figure {ch4-perspective-1906}.** "Perspective map showing route of Lafe Pence's electric line from Lewis and Clark
Exposition grounds to Linnton": the fairgrounds marked as the "factory and mill district" and a "residence district of
North Portland", Guild's Lake, Willamette Heights, and the line running north along the St. Helens road to Linnton.
Morning Oregonian, 4 May 1906, p. 11.
[Catalogue entry](cat:news-oregonian-1906-05-04-p11-perspective-map-pence-electric-line-linnton)
:::

By spring the plan had grown into a whole district. The County Court gave the Pence Co. a franchise for an electric
line from the fairgrounds to Linnton (Figure {ch4-perspective-1906})
([Oregonian, 4 May 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-05-04-p11-perspective-map-pence-electric-line-linnton)). On 22 May
he bought the Government Building and its annexes on the peninsula for $3,000, and arranged with the Reed estate, which
owned the ground, to fill the peninsula in return for the use of the land
([Oregonian, 23 May 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-05-23-p12-pence-buys-government-building)); a day later he was
offering the building to a condensed-milk company
([Oregonian, 24 May 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-05-24-p10-condensed-milk-factory-government-building)). In the
Made in Oregon parade the Portland Development Co.'s float showed the fair buildings as factories, with busts of
McMillin and Pence ([Oregonian, 25 May 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-05-25-p10-made-in-oregon-parade-pence-float)).
By the autumn the buildings belonged to McMillin's company
([Oregonian, 8 Oct 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-10-08-p14-pence-returns-flume-for-orn-ties)), and Pence's part of
the fair was the ground he meant to make: the Cottel tract and the fill behind the Government Building, both waiting
on the hill.

<div class="keynum"><span>$5,525</span> for state buildings that had cost $290,000; $3,000 more for the Government
Building.</div>

## 5. Washing down the Scotch Nubbin

<p class="when">Winter 1906-07</p>

::: figure ch5-flume-picture.jpg
**Figure {ch5-flume-picture}.** "System of flumes with which Lafe Pence is sluicing down great hills and filling Guild's Lake."
Looking north, down Balch Gulch: the cut face on the Nubbin at left, the flumes zig-zagging down the gulch
wall, the Thurman Street bridge at right and the Government Building across the lake. The photograph is retouched
(Mount St. Helens is drawn in). Morning Oregonian, 11 March 1907, p. 12.
[Catalogue entry](cat:news-oregonian-1907-03-11-p12-pences-dreams-coming-true)
:::

The Scotch Nubbin was a knoll on the Willamette Heights side of Balch Gulch, about 430 feet high. What is left of it
is the house-topped knob that stands at 374 feet today: the knoll's summit, cut down by some 55 feet. It is the great dome in the 1906 drawing (Figure {ch3-birdseye}), and Pence's giants had been at its back face since
early 1906; in November 1906 he
went at it in earnest. On the 27th a 400-pound
black-powder blast, set in two tunnels, rattled windows all over Willamette Heights. The residents protested, and the
next day City Engineer Taylor revoked Pence's blasting permit
([Journal, 28 Nov 1906](cat:news-oregon-journal-1906-11-28-p1-taylor-revokes-pence-blasting-permit)). Pence said he
would keep washing the hill down anyway; it would only cost more
([Oregonian, 28 Nov 1906](cat:news-oregonian-1906-11-28-p10-will-disregard-kickers-pence-blasting);
[Journal, 29 Nov 1906](cat:news-oregon-journal-1906-11-29-p9-pence-continues-scotch-nubbin)). Across the river, the
St. Johns Review joked that its babies had slept through the blast
([St. Johns Review, 30 Nov 1906](cat:news-st-johns-review-1906-11-30-p3-pence-little-blast-quip)).

Through the winter three hydraulic giants washed away the upper part of the knoll. The spoil ran off in box flumes on
trestles down the north wall of the gulch, across it near Savier and 31st, past Pence's office in the old Emergency
Hospital, through the Klondike building and out over the Trail and the Bridge of Nations: nearly a mile, to a diked fill
behind the Government Building (Figure {ch5-route-map})
([Oregonian, 7 Mar 1907](https://oregonnews.uoregon.edu/lccn/sn83025138/1907-03-07/ed-1/seq-12/)). The Journal
counted some 35 miles of flume on three levels across the whole works
([Journal, 8 Mar 1907](cat:news-oregon-journal-1907-03-08-p5-pence-filling-terminals-35-miles-flumes)).

::: figure ch5-route-map.jpg narrow
**Figure {ch5-route-map}.** The spoil route. The cut face and the flumes on the hill are traced from the 11 March 1907 picture
(Figure {ch5-flume-picture}); below the junction the line follows the route the Oregonian described on 7 March, with the gulch crossing
where the trestle fell. The crossing is the only point a source pins down; the rest is the most direct line that keeps

:::

The work was dangerous. On 15 February 1907 a new section of trestle, about 40 feet above the gulch near Savier
Street, gave way under the men building it. H. B. Purdin, 42, lately of Hillsboro, died that afternoon; Elmer Thompson
and Thomas Jewell were badly hurt (Figure {ch5-trestle-collapse})
([Journal, 15 Feb 1907](cat:news-oregon-journal-1907-02-15-p2-buried-in-debris-flume-trestle-collapse);
[Oregonian, 16 Feb 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-02-16-p10-workmen-carried-down-with-bridge-balchs-gulch)). Two weeks
later a rock sluiced down by Pence's crew struck a policeman in Macleay Park
([Journal, 3 Mar 1907](cat:news-oregon-sunday-journal-1907-03-03-s2-p30-pence-rock-hits-policeman-macl)).

::: figure ch5-trestle-collapse.jpg narrow
**Figure {ch5-trestle-collapse}.** "Scene of fatal accident in Balch's Gulch": the two trestle towers still standing either side of the

On 11 March the Oregonian printed a picture of the whole system (Figure {ch5-flume-picture}). It credited the winter with about
200,000 cubic yards washed into the lake and a 22-acre tract filling behind the Government Building, and Pence
promised to finish the Nubbin by dry grading by August, then start on the east side of the gulch
([Oregonian, 11 Mar 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-03-11-p12-pences-dreams-coming-true)).

Matched to a model of the ground before the cuts, seen from the same spot, the hill in the picture fills the same
outline as the knoll, and the cut face is eaten into its flank, while the flumes run where the gulch wall lets them
(Figure {ch5-picture-vs-model}). The picture's crest already sits some 30 to 40 feet below the model's summit: a
winter of washing down the top. Much of what the model shows in blue was still standing in March 1907.

Not everyone saw progress. Writing for the Willamette Heights residents, Jerry Bronaugh said the company had
"scarcely scratched" the Nubbin
([Oregonian, 15 Mar 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-03-15-p8-bronaugh-letter-its-up-to-pence-company)). In May,
Russell & Blyth, who by then owned the land around the knoll, hired George McCabe's steam shovels to take it down;
by late June the shovels were on the hill and the hydraulic work had stopped
([Oregonian, 9 May 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-05-09-p12-russell-blyth-steam-shovel-scotch-nubbin-blyt);
[Oregonian, 23 Jun 1907](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1907-06-23-s3-p32-scotch-nubbin-steam-shovels)).

<div class="keynum"><span>About 200,000 cu yd</span> washed into Guild's Lake that winter, out of roughly 470,000 the
knoll has lost in all (estimated from the model). One man killed.</div>

## 6. The shovels, and the money runs out

<p class="when">1907-1909</p>

The steam shovels finished what the giants had started. McCabe's contract was to move 275,000 cubic yards, and the
spoil was to level Russell & Blyth's own lots within 600 or 700 feet of the hill, not to go to the lake
([Oregonian, 9 May 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-05-09-p12-russell-blyth-steam-shovel-scotch-nubbin-blyt)). The
hydraulic work had been given up because the ground was too hard to wash
([Oregonian, 23 Jun 1907](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1907-06-23-s3-p32-scotch-nubbin-steam-shovels)). By then the giants
had already taken some 40 feet off the top: the hill's outline in the March picture sits no higher than about 391 feet,
against roughly 429 on the 1896 survey. The shovels took the summit down to today's 374 feet and cut back its west side
(Figure {ch6-knoll-section}).

::: figure ch6-knoll-section.jpg
**Figure {ch6-knoll-section}.** A west-east section through the Scotch Nubbin. Dashed: the knoll before any cutting,
from the 1896 USGS survey. Shaded band: where the ground could have stood in March 1907, between today's surface and
the highest it can be given the hill's outline in the 11 March picture. Solid: today. Made for this story from the
USGS 1896 Portland quadrangle (Olmsted job #2640 plan 6), the picture's solved camera and the 2019 lidar.

Pence's own trouble was money. In May the Park Board asked him to show why his Macleay Park flume permit should not be
revoked, citing damage and dying ferns
([Oregonian, 4 May 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-05-04-p12-park-board-flumes-not-wanted)); he asked to keep the
flumes another year, admitting a dam had broken in the winter
([Oregonian, 9 Jun 1907](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1907-06-09-p8-pence-asks-flumes-stay-macleay-another-)). That
summer he still talked of buying the whole fairground for $300,000 and of filling 22 acres round the Government
Building the next winter
([Oregonian, 4 Aug 1907](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1907-08-04-p8-pence-option-fair-grounds-300000)), and then turned
to selling firewood to break the wood trust
([Journal, 20 Aug 1907](cat:news-oregon-journal-1907-08-20-p1-pence-timber-cord-wood-trust)), a deal the unions
said he never delivered
([Labor Press, 23 Sep 1907](cat:news-portland-labor-press-1907-09-23-p1-pence-cannot-connect-wood)).

Then the banks went (Figure {ch6-money-timeline}). Oregon Trust & Savings failed in August 1907, the first Portland failure of that year's panic;
its receiver found Pence owing $8,716 on 1905 loans secured by his Willamette Heights hydraulic plant
([Oregonian, 18 Sep 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-09-18-p14-devlin-receiver-report-oregon-trust-pence-not)). Among
the depositors was Purdin's widow, who lost nearly $1,000
([Hillsboro Argus, 29 Aug 1907](cat:news-hillsboro-argus-1907-08-29-p1-purdin-widow-loses-savings-oregon-trust)).
On 28 October Title Guarantee & Trust, Pence's main lender, closed its doors. The Oregonian put the failure of his
fairground schemes first among the causes: $225,000 lent to him and $175,000 more sunk in land
([Oregonian, 7 Nov 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-11-07-p10-title-guarantee-receiver-pence-loans)). Pence answered that
his company owed the bank nothing, its debt being to the bank's subsidiary, the Colonial Investment Co.
([Oregonian, 8 Nov 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-11-08-p10-pence-does-not-owe-wrecked-bank)). The paper asked how he
had got "a credit of a quarter of a million" in the first place
([Oregonian, 26 Nov 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-11-26-p8-editorial-all-should-understand-it-pence-credi)), and the
bonding company's controller put it plainly: the people's savings "were flushed into the side of a hill to fill up
fair grounds" ([Oregonian, 9 Dec 1907](cat:news-oregonian-1907-12-09-p9-surety-lafrentz-pence-loans-hillside)).

::: figure ch6-money-timeline.jpg
**Figure {ch6-money-timeline}.** The money, 1905-1909: what the banks and the papers said Pence's companies owed, and

The examiners' report in February 1908 found the bank had never been solvent and that "the Lafe Pence companies owe the
bank $399,000" ([Oregonian, 8 Feb 1908](cat:news-oregonian-1908-02-08-p1-experts-report-title-guarantee-pence-399000));
Pence wrote back that his loan was $220,000
([Oregonian, 9 Feb 1908](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1908-02-09-s3-p7-pence-letter-loan-is-220000)). By August he was
in court over unpaid judgments, accusing the bank's officers of plotting to ruin him, and handed over four silver
dollars ([Oregonian, 21 Aug 1908](cat:news-oregonian-1908-08-21-p11-pence-says-ross-tried-to-ruin-him)). The city
could not make him take the park flume out before June 1909
([Journal, 8 Mar 1909](cat:news-oregon-journal-1909-03-08-p3-city-cannot-oust-pence-flume-macleay-park)); by the end
of that month it was gone
([Journal, 29 Jun 1909](cat:news-oregon-journal-1909-06-29-p6-pence-gets-check-back-flume-removed)). In November,
reporting Lewis & Wiley's purchase of the land, the Oregonian noted that Pence had tried "this identical project"
and failed ([Oregonian, 21 Nov 1909](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1909-11-21-s4-p7-lewis-wiley-buy-90-acres-guilds-lake)).

<div class="keynum"><span>$220,000 to $399,000</span> of bank money behind the scheme, depending on who was counting;
two banks failed in 1907 with it on their books.</div>

## 7. What's left

<p class="when">1909 to today</p>

In November 1909 Lewis & Wiley, the Seattle regrade contractors, bought 90 acres of Guild's Lake land to fill, and the
Oregonian noted that Pence had attempted "this identical project" and failed after building his flumes and starting to
sluice ([Oregonian, 21 Nov 1909](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1909-11-21-s4-p7-lewis-wiley-buy-90-acres-guilds-lake)).
They pumped their water instead of waiting for winter rain, and over five years washed Goldsmith Hill and the slopes
behind it into the lake as Westover Terraces. When W. H. Lewis declared the work finished in 1914, he said every trace
of construction had been cleared away, "even including the old Lafe Pence structures"
([Oregonian, 13 Sep 1914](cat:news-sunday-oregonian-1914-09-13-s4-p8-westover-hill-removed-diagram)). That is the
next story.

The bank's receiver wrote Pence's accounts off as worthless in 1912
([Oregonian, 27 Jan 1912](cat:news-oregonian-1912-01-27-p9-title-receiver-report-pence-worthless)). By then he was a
patent lawyer in Washington, D.C., turning up as a contested delegate to that year's Democratic convention
([Journal, 6 Jun 1912](cat:news-oregon-journal-1912-06-06-p19-pence-bobs-up-dc-delegate-baltimore)); a year later his
wife sued him for separation
([Oregonian, 17 Jun 1913](cat:news-oregonian-1913-06-17-p3-mrs-pence-sues-lillian-duke)). He died in Washington in
October 1923 and was buried in Columbus, Indiana (Biographical Directory of the U.S. Congress).

On the ground, the Scotch Nubbin is still there if you know where to look (Figure {ch7-today-map}). The house-topped
knob at 374 feet, west of NW 32nd and 33rd Avenues, is its summit, cut down by some 55 feet; the low ground just west
of it is the small valley that lay behind the knoll on the 1896 survey. The steep face above Balch Gulch is where the
giants and then the shovels bit into it, and the gulch floor by the Thurman Street bridge, which Pence's trestles
crossed, now lies under as much as 40 feet of later fill.
